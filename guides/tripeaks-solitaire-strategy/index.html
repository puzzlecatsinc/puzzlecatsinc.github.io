TriPeaks Solitaire Strategy: Plan Runs, Reveals and Stock Draws

Practical TriPeaks solitaire strategy: compare visible routes, uncover useful cards, manage the stock and check rule differences before chasing a streak.

TriPeaks solitaire gives you a small decision with every card: take the obvious move, or choose a route that opens more of the board? A useful strategy is to compare the next few visible moves before committing. Long runs are satisfying, but revealing a blocked card or saving a useful connection can matter more than extending a streak.

Check the rules of your TriPeaks version

The usual move is to remove an exposed tableau card one rank above or below the top waste card. A 7 can accept a 6 or an 8, regardless of suit. Clearing the tableau is the goal. When no useful move is available, a stock draw changes the waste card. Details differ: this playable TriPeaks ruleset, for example, changes King–Ace wrapping and card visibility between difficulty settings.

Before planning a run, check three things in your own game: whether Kings connect to Aces, whether covered cards are visible, and whether the stock can be reused. Also read how streaks and stock draws affect scoring. A strategy for maximizing a score can differ from a strategy for clearing a difficult board.

Compare routes, not just individual cards

Trace the visible continuation

Suppose the waste is a 7 and both a 6 and an 8 are exposed. If an accessible 5 can follow the 6, that side offers a visible two-card route. If removing the 8 would unlock a covered card, it offers new information instead. Neither option is automatically better: compare the cards you can see, the stock remaining and the objective of this hand.

Do not count a covered card as available just because its rank would fit. Trace the covering relationships as well as the ranks. A promising sequence is useful only if each card is exposed when you need it.

Notice the cards that connect two routes

An exposed card can be a bridge. For example, after moving from a 7 down to a 6, another 7 may let you turn upward toward an 8. Spending that second 7 earlier could remove the bridge. When two cards of the same rank are playable, compare what each one reveals before choosing between them.

Use reveals as a tiebreaker

If two choices have similar visible continuations, favoring the one that uncovers a card is a reasonable heuristic. It gives you another possible move and, in hidden-card versions, more information. It is not a guarantee: the newly revealed card might be unusable. Avoid treating “always uncover first” as a rule that overrides every visible alternative.

Make each stock draw deliberate

Before drawing, rescan the exposed cards in both rank directions. It is easy to overlook a descending move while following an ascending run. If a legal route remains, compare taking it with the consequences of drawing under your game’s scoring rules.

In a one-pass version, every draw uses a limited opportunity to change rank. Near the end, think about the ranks your remaining cards need. With a waste 4 and an exposed 9, for instance, you need another connection; repeatedly tapping the 9 will not make it legal. This sounds simple, but naming the missing connection helps explain why a hand became stuck.

Learn from each hand

If your version offers undo, replay a fork to see what the alternative reveals. Treat that as a learning exercise; a replay informed by hidden cards is different from solving the original deal. Keep scoring comparisons within the same ruleset, and separate clearing the board from achieving a high score.

Hidden cards and the stock order limit what you can know. A lost hand does not prove every earlier choice was wrong. A more useful review question is: “Was there an exposed route I missed, or did I knowingly choose a reveal over a longer run?”

TriPeaks questions

Can you play an Ace on a King?

Only when your version allows wrapping. Check the in-game rules before planning a route across that boundary.

Should you clear all three peaks evenly?

Balance is a useful prompt to inspect neglected cards, rather than a requirement. A route through one peak can still be the best available way to open the board.

Where can I try a scenic TriPeaks game?

Solitaire Explorer: Tripeaks pairs TriPeaks boards with journey-map progression through natural settings. Its official iPhone and iPad listing describes readable cards and changing destinations. Check its in-game rules for the precise scoring and card-play details.
